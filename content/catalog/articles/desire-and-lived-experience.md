::text{id="distance-opening"}

You want an evening with Ana. A table somewhere, a long conversation, the kind of laugh that only happens in person. A desire sparks, and a lived experience waits at the other end.

Everything in between is distance: calls, messages, forms, maps, waiting. For as long as there have been designers, they have worked in that space. Designers were designing the distance between desire and lived experience.

The evening itself has barely changed in thirty-five years. The distance to it keeps changing shape.

## The same evening, every era {#distance-eras}

Around 1990, you ring Ana on a landline and check a paper diary. Then you find the restaurant in the phone book and ring again. Designers shaped the keypad, the diary page and the directory listing.

Around 2000, a desktop computer joins the phone. You email Ana, look up the restaurant's website and print a map for the journey. Designers made the machine legible with windows, icons and a pointer.

Around 2005, you book online through a web form: fields, dropdowns, a captcha, a pop-up offering a newsletter, a confirmation email. The interface sits at its peak, and so does the friction.

Around 2012, the smartphone puts it all in your pocket. You message Ana through instant messaging, "does Thursday work?", book in an app and follow a map app to the door. Designers spend a decade removing steps on purpose.

Today, the pieces are connected into systems. Calendars sync across phone, watch and laptop, notifications follow you, and AI assistants can suggest a place. Arranging the evening still means moving between a messenger, a calendar, a booking app and a map.

Next, you think dinner with Ana next week, and an agent does the rest. What reaches you is one line: Thursday, 19:30. Confirmed. This part has started to arrive, and it isn't everyday life yet.

The evening at the end of every row stays the same. Only the row gets shorter.

## The interface was a map of the distance {#distance-map}

Read the eras side by side and the interface looks like a map of the distance at each moment: every keypad, window, form and app marks where the gap between wanting and having stood that year.

Design research named this gap long ago. In 1986, Ed Hutchins, Jim Hollan and Don Norman described the gulf of execution, the effort of turning a goal into actions a system accepts, and the gulf of evaluation, the effort of understanding what the system did (NN/g). Every era since has been narrowing those gulfs.

## Agents will nearly close it {#distance-agents}

With an agent, the gulf of execution shrinks to a single thought. Your agent checks your calendar, asks Ana's agent about hers, books the table and plans the route.

The people who built the screen era are already working toward this. Sam Altman has said he wants OpenAI's devices to break the "unquestioned assumptions" of smart devices, starting with screens (Inc.).

## When the distance hides a mistake {#distance-mistake}

Now imagine that future Thursday arrives and Ana isn't there. The agent booked the right restaurant for the wrong week, or picked the place she quietly dislikes. Every step happened out of sight, so the mistake did too.

The gulf of evaluation has just widened. You can no longer see how the evening was arranged, only whether it went well.

## So what's left for designers? {#distance-question}

The distance between desire and lived experience is closing faster than ever. Designers have spent decades shortening it, one keypad, window, form and app at a time. When it reaches almost nothing, and the few moments that remain happen out of sight, what is left for a designer to do?
